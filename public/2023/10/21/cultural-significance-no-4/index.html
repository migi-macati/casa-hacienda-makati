Cultural Significance No. 4

Originally published 2023-10-21

The Casa Hacienda of Makati is the embodiment of Makati.

Embodiment - the dictionary defines “to embody” as “making visible,” and that is exactly what the Casa Hacienda do for Makati’s identity as a hacienda or a social enterprise. The relic of the Casa Hacienda makes visible all of what Makati City is: its history, development, aspirations, culture, and heritage.

Hacienda Makati or Hacienda de San Pedro Makati was founded in 1607 (more than 300 years ago!) by Don Pedro de Brito to support the formation of Jesuit novices. The endowment included the land for the Novitiate House dedicated to St. Peter the Apostle with a church for the spiritual care of the natives. More importantly, the income from the hacienda helped sustain the Jesuit mission in the country even after the Novitiate was abandoned two decades after.

When the Jesuits were expelled from Spanish colonies in 1769, Hacienda Makati was confiscated by the King and was subsequently acquired by Don Pedro de Galarraga, the Marquis of Villamediana, in 1793. Even with the successive owners, the tradition of service started by Don Pedro de Brito continues to this day as evidenced by their works and contributions to local and national development.

Luciano P.R. Santiago summarizes the concept of the hacienda succinctly, "Modern Filipinos tend to loosely apply the term "hacienda" to any big land area, whether real or imagined. But during the Spanish Regime, the huge landholding that was the hacienda was a formally organized estate, officially recognized as such by the colonial government and regulated by particular laws of the Indies. It was formed by royal grant, purchase or donation. As a system, it had a casa hacienda where lived the owner or his representative; a titular saint with a church administered by a resident chaplain (cura capellan) who was often a Filipino priest appointed by the bishop; a supporting cast of inquilinos (tenants) who were mostly gentlemen farmers who lived near the casa; and a legion of casamas or reservas (farmer-laborers) who constituted, though they were not acknowledged as, the backbone of the estate. The hacienda was the microcosm of colonial society at large." (Don Pasqual de Sta. Ana (1762–1827), Indio Hacendero, Philippine Studies vol. 50, no. 1 (2002): 23–49)

The economic output of the hacienda was accounted for by the administrator of the estate in 1769 as follows: “Hacienda Makati had an area of at least four-square miles located in a fertile plain. It had 92 head of cattle, 14 horses, 63 carabaos and a tile and pottery factory. The granary contained 385 cavans of palay and another 119 cavans set aside for sowing. Its administrator, Vicente Geronimo Cabrera, received eight percent of its annual income as salary.” At the same time, Governor Anda reported that its pottery factory netted an income of P30,000 a year. (Una Memoria de Anda y Salazar, 1899)

The hacienda has 1,009 inhabitants in 1773.

A century after, Agustinian missionaries describe the hacienda is as follows: "The first San Pedro Macati, located in the boundaries of the old province of Tondo but situated in the municipality of Macati, constituted of farmland and pasturage for cattle and horses. There was a central residence, ovens for baking lime, roof-tiles, and brick, and a tropiche for milling sugar-cane. After the Jesuits were expelled, Don Pedro de Galarraga, Marquis of Villamediana, acquired it at a public auction. He tried to improve it at great expense to himself, renovating, maintaining or improving not only the house, but also the irrigation dyke, the pottery shop, as well as by an act of generosity, the parochial church." (Diccionario Geográfico, Estadístico, Histórico, de las Islas Filipinas, vol. 2, Manuel y Bravo Buzeta, O.S.A., 1850-1851)

What is left of the Casa Hacienda today is a shell of a building fully utilized by its owners then left to obscurity. The city that inherited its name and nature to the hacienda would benefit greatly by rediscovering its forgotten heritage through the appreciation of the Casa Hacienda that embodies a large part of its identity. Preservation would naturally come after by the will of the community.

Meanwhile, Hacienda Makati survives to this day with the private ownership of the Makati Commercial Estate and other master-planned communities in the city, and with the estate model of development that has since spread across the country. When we think of Makati City today, we recall the office and residential skyscrapers, manicured parks, first-rate infrastructure, and the malls mostly within these private estates. All these traces its roots back to the founding of the Hacienda Makati, embodied by its Casa Hacienda.
