History of Barrio Olimpia, Makati, Rizal

Originally published 2023-10-21

Excerpt on Barrio Olimpia, now Barangay Olympia, from the Report on History and Cultural Life of the Barrio Olimpia, Makati, Rizal docketed in the History and Cultural Life of Makati

  1. The official name of the barrio is Olimpia, one which was adapted through a resolution of the Municipal Council in 1925, headed by the then Municipal President, Nicanor G. Garcia1.
  1. (a) Popular Names

The barrio presently known as OLIMPIA, a name originating from the trade mark of the products of a tile and brick factory which once stood alongside the Pasig River, near the present Lissar & Co. Building2 commonly known as Tabing-Ilog.

In the past the barrio was known as "HINERO", a tagalog term short for "Inginiero". The name became popular those days because a German engineer was then living in the barrio. The man was popular because he was the only engineer in the community and at the same time a German.

(b) The sites comprising the barrio are: Sampalukan in the north; Valenzuala in the southern part; Obrero in the east; Olimpia proper in the West; and Paltok in the north western area.

  1. The barrio was officially established in 1935 during the incumbency of the then Municipal President, Nicanor G. Garcia. Before then, it was only known to be a portion of Barrio Matungaw, now Tejeros.

(c) The Oldest Cemetery in the Suburbs

The English Cemetery, the oldest cemetery in the suburbs of Manila, is situated at the end of the street bearing its name and adjacent to the Manila South Cemetery, was constructed by the English colony sometime in the middle of the 19th century (1860-65), the wall surrounding which are one meter thick and 5 meters high. These served as an ideal fortress for the Filipino revolutionists in 1898 against the Spaniards and again in 1899 against the Americans.

The famous walls were destroyed by the Japanese in 1943, and the strones, which were extraordinary in size, were used to build the trenches and air-raid shelters for their soldiers in Sakura Hei, now Fort McKinley.

It was from this point also that the American Liberation Forces first began bombarding enemy positions in the southern part of Manila, causing the early surrender of the Japanese in the area.

  1. Important Facts, Incidents, or Events That Took Place

1898

Battle against the Spanish Army, the Filipino revolutionists led by General Pio del Pilar and Major Carmona.

1899

Battle against the Americans - The Filipinos led by General Pio del Pilar.

1925

The establishment of Barrio Olimpia and this was during the administration of the then Municipal President Nicanor G. Garcia.

1935

The first barrio school building was constructed in Paltok by the government through the initiative of the then Municipal President, Jose D. Villena.

1935-36

It was during this period that the Philippine Racing Club was constructed, the opening of which contributed a great deal to the advancement of the economic and the social life of the Barrio. Mr. John Cancoon Sr., on whose initiative the project became a reality, was also the controlling stockholder of the corporation.

1943-44

Eighty Filipino prisoners of war from Bataan and Corregidor were captured in the Luzon Cabaret and the Manila South Cemetery and trained by the Japanese "Yu En2" or the General Service Troop. "Ganap" members (a Filipino Pro-Japanese organization) also enlisted here. These men were released in batch in July and August of 1944 and the last batch was abandoned in the latter part of the same year. They realized that they were no match to the nearing enemy. A skeleton force of the Japanese intructors and sentinels did not abandon their posts in the vicinity of the Nielson Airfield where they were trapped and wiped out by the guerillas in February, 1945.

January 6, 1945

Two waves of B-24's and B-29's dropped fragmentary bombs on almost all sections of the Barrio after the U.S. Forces have received the intelligence report that Japanese military men and civilians occupied several houses in the community. As a result of this raid about 30 dead and wounded including one Japanese officer were listed. About 50 houses and big buildings were either damaged or razed to the ground.

February 8, 1945

Sections of Sacramento and English Cemetery streets were bombed by three U.S. dive bombers releasing three heavy bombs, and as a result of this raid, four families were nearly wiped out and their houses razed to the ground beyond repair.

In the evening the American batterymen from the other side of the Pasig River shelled a part of Sitio Sampalukan. A number of dead and wounded were rushed to the Army Hospital in Mandaluyong for treatment, one of them being Colonel Bejamin Molina, the USAFFE major. A number of houses in Calle Malolos were burned and some along the Provincial highway were destroyed.

February 9, 1945

The Liberation Forces first set foot on Makati soil on the banks of the Pasig River near the Lizzar & Co.3 and the present site of the Manila Foundry.4

February 9, 1946

The first anniversary of the Liberation of the Barrio was celebrated and the guest of honor for the occasion was Doña Trinidad L. de Roxas, wife of the then candidate for the Presidency of the Commonweaalth of the Philippines, General Manuel Roxas. Led by Mr. Pedro Calasanz, Captain Vicente Martelino and Gregorio Segovia, and several other known civic-spitited citizens, one of them Mr. Quirino Reyes, the celebration was the most colorful of its kind since the establishment of the barrio.

1947

Establishment of Sitio Valenzuela, thru the insistence of the residents led by the Barrio Lieutenants, Feliciano Villareal and Perfecto Figueroa, and under the strength of a resilution sponsored by the then Councelor for Olimpia, Pedro Calasanz. The then Municipal Mayor, Pablo C. Cotez, inagurated its establishment.

  1. (a) Destruction of lives, properties, etc.

During the last few years of the Spanish regime (1896-1900), there were only a few inhabitants in the communities now comprising the barrio of Olimpia. The place was mostly zacate fields and the nearest neighbor would be a kilometer or more away. Therefore, nothing much worth knowing can be mentioned as regards the destruction of lives, properties and institutions of the inhabitants, except a few hundreds of soliders (Filipinos, Spaniards, and Americans) who fought in the place.

In 1944-45, the barrio was already thickly populated. The strategical location of the barrio, in view of the conversion of Neilson Airfield into a Japanese military air-base and the Philippine Racing Club into a Japanese Quartermaster Depot, caused a great havoc to the residents. People, whose only means of livelihood was their employment with these Japanese installations, expose their lives to the American planes from above and the temptation to steal. Underfed laborers gamble their lives by pilfering Japanese owned goodstuffs and war materials. As a result, not less than 100 residents are known to have perished through horrible ways of death, after they were caught and tortured.

In view of the position (Nielson Airfield is located in the southern part of the barrio and the Philippine Racing Club way up in the north), the Japanese Military men occupied the biggest house in the barrio. Intelligence report therefore related their position to the U.S. Forces who raided not less than 200 persons died inside and outside these two Japanes camps.

About 200 persons, more or less, perished of malnutrition or famine, and this was due to the total abandonment of large scale farming in the area.

It may however be consoling to mention that a number of cases (about 10 to 15) had been officially registered as natural deaths or missing, when in fact the natural causes of which point their collaboration with the Japanese.

The Nielson Airfield, formerly a commercial air-base, the Philippine Racing Club and several other residential and commercial buildings suffered considerable destructions. Farmers also suffered heavy losses of crops, work-animals, and farm [illegible] at the hands of the Japanese, their Filipino henchmen and local theives.

(b) Measures and Accomplishments toward rehabilitation and reconstruction following World War II.

People from all walks of life whose properties were either damaged, lost or destroyed as a result of the past war, in 1946 when the Damage Law was approved, grabbed the opportunity by filling their WD Claims with the WDC. And in 1948, most of them were able to reconstruct their business establishments and residential houses. It was also through this benefit that the government was able to reconstruct the Provincial roads which passed through the main portion of the barrio.

People whose houses were either burned or sold during the Japanese occupation period, built temporary buildings (barong-barongs) out of waste materials given out to the poor by the U.S. Military Camp. Some of them built on their lands and squatted on unoccupied lots owned by the Hacienda Ayala.
