Cultural Significance No. 6

Originally published 2023-10-21

The Casa Hacienda of Makati was the site of the First Philippine Porcelain Factory.

The year is 1903. Imagine Manila and its surroundings just a few years after the revolution, and are now facing the uncertainties brought about by the US takeover of the Spanish colonial government.

Enrique Jacobo Pedro Luis Plácido Zóbel de Ayala (Don Enrique for short), who was then an owner and manager of Ayala y Compania, founded La Porcelanica, the first Philippine porcelain manufacturing company in 1903 and placed it under the direction of Francisco Quintos.

It was in their wholly-owned hacienda in Makati, renowned for its clay products or ceramics, in the site of the Casa Hacienda along J. Bonifacio [Roxas] Street, that they built a small factory with a Japanese-style wood-heated furnace. With the help of Japanese master-potters and painter-decorators, together with their Filipino apprentices, they were able to produce porcelains starting 1904. These products ranged from plates, flower pots, jars, cups, tea sets to refractory bricks and porcelain insulators.

To note, on February 19, 1906, the Hacienda de San Pedro Macati was registered in accordance with the provisions of the Land Registration Act, together with the building "constructed of strong materials", called the Casa-Quinta or Casa de Ingenieros. The latter may be in reference to the use of the main building to accommodate the factory workers.

It is also good to note that one of the two known photographs of the Casa Hacienda was taken during this time in 1910. Following the ceramic tradition, the colonnades are seen to be made of bricks while tiles are used for the roof.

Unfortunately, Don Enrique's foray into porcelain came to an end after only 8 years of its establishment. The factory was dismantled in 1911 after operations showed consistent losses from excessive production costs.

This endeavor into porcelain production by Don Enrique may seem limited and isolated. However, we should look at it in view of the long clay pottery tradition of indigenous Filipinos and the pre-Hispanic fascination of locals for Chinese porcelain.

The Jesuits were recorded to have assessed Hacienda Makati’s suitability for manufacturing clay products. Succeeding owners and tenants of the hacienda continued the tradition until the hacienda and town became widely known for the production of pottery, ceramic utensils, construction bricks and roof tiles.

According to the City Profile of the Makati LGU, the soil type in most areas of the city has a high composition of clay in the surface level and a low Manganese level. Both attributes contribute to very low permeability and poor drainage, making the area prone to flooding. This also makes the soil a suitable material for clay.

Today, only traces remain of this ceramic tradition for appreciation of observant eyes. The names of two barangays are directly related to ceramics: Tejeros, which is Spanish for roof tile makers or people who make roof tiles (tejas) and bricks; and Olympia, named after the prominent tile and brick factory.

Prominent public buildings in Makati City give homage to this tradition by having brick finish even just superficially: Central Fire Station and fire substations, Central Police Station, public schools, and other community facilities. There is however no design guide or any write-up to confirm this connection.

Finally, now lost are the ceramic jar finials, which can only be seen in old photos showing the pinnacles of the San Pedro Macati Church and the wall posts of the San Pedro Macati Cemetery now Plaza Cristo Rey.

Further studies can be made on the role of Hacienda Makati as embodied by its Casa Hacienda in the history of ceramics in the Philippines. At the very least, the short-lived legacy of La Porcelanica established within the site of the Casa Hacienda serves as a glimpse of the peak of the local ceramic heritage of Makati.

Note: This particular post made use of inference to the best explanation on the location of the La Porcelanica factory with the limited data available: (1) the identification of the street where it was established - J. Bonifacio Street, (2) the 1909 map showing San Pedro Macati and the existing streets with and without street names, and (3) the mention of the Casa de Ingeneros and its compound in the 1907 Supreme Court decision as the only notable improvement owned by the owners of the hacienda.
