Cultural Significance No. 11

Originally published 2024-02-06

The Casa Hacienda of Makati was Ground Zero of the Liberation of Makati.

The first river crossing where the Japanese least expected it was near the Philippine Racing Club, specifically at the Casa Hacienda compound, then used and more known as the Lissar factory,1 in Barrio Olimpia. This marks the first landing of the Liberation Forces in Makati, and will be commemorated as the Liberation of Barrio Olimpia 2, and the start of the Battle and Liberation of Makati (February 9 – 12, 1945).

February 9, 1945 - First Landing in Makati

On February 9, 1945, after shelling the encampments in the south side of the Pasig River, the 8th Cavalry Regiment of the 1st Cavalry Division, dubbed the Flying Columns, commanded by Brig. Gen. Verne D. Mudge under of the XIV Corps, Sixth US Army crossed the river from Mandaluyong to Makati.

Using a pontoon bridge, they landed in Makati to meet with the forces from Batangas and Cavite and to encircle the Japanese positions along Manila Bay in Pasay, Ermita, Malate, and Intramuros.

“The cavalry encountered practically no opposition in the crossing area, but progressed slowly because the Japanese had thoroughly mined many of the streets south and west of the [Philippine Racing Club].”

“On 9 and 10 February, units of the 1st Cavalry Division crossed the Pasig River and fanned out in three directions-toward Manila Bay on the west, Neilson Field on the south, and Fort McKinley on the southeast.” 3

The Battle of Manila, 3 February - 3 March 1945 (Plate No. 78); Reports of General MacArthur: The Campaigns of MacArthur in the Pacific, Vol. 1, p. 274; accessed on November 2, 2023 at: https://history.army.mil/html/books/013/13-3/CMH_Pub_13-3.pdf

A first hand account of the momentous event was recorded by a group of Filipino guerillas called the Santa Ana Vigilantes: “That night and the following morning the Philippine Racing Club and “Lissar” were heavily shelled by the Americans. The following afternoon the forces of democracy landed on our side of the Pasig river. Capt. Jose and his trusted leaders in delegation representing the Santa Ana Vigilantes met the 1st Cavalry to offer Col. Bradley the services of the Santa Ana Vigillantes and all able bodies residents of the district who were ready and willing to go with the U.S. Army and help annihilate the enemy.” 4

Similarly on 10 February, the 5th Cavalry Regiment of the 1st Cavalry Division, crossing the river using native bancas to the Makati town proper, “met no ground opposition, but considerable machine gun and mortar fire, originating from the Fort McKinley area.” 5

February 12, 1945 - Liberation of Makati

By the 12 February, the rest of the 1st Cavalry has liberated the Makati town proper and the Makati electrical power substation (in what is now the Rockwell Center), the Neilson Airfield, Culi-culi (now Barrangay Pio del Pilar), and the shores of Manila Bay south of what is now Buendia Ave. “The Japanese abandoned Neilson Field without a struggle.” 6

This swift maneuver completed the encirclement of Japanese forces in Manila, leaving them no option to retreat. Some war analysts consider this as one of the factors that tragically lead the Japanese Manila Naval Defence Force to fight to the death. 7

The Casa Hacienda's Strategic Location

The Japanese requisitioned the Casa Hacienda sometime in 1944 to be used as their ordnance shop.8 9 It was noted that the ordnance shop from the Nichols and Nielson Airfields were transferred to the Lissar factory. 10 This was probably to save their supplies from more obvious military targets with the benefit of having large and sturdy storage facilities.

Meanwhile, the Philippine Racing Club was used as Quartermaster Depot while Nielson Airfield was used as a military airfield.

The three largest establishments were at the edge of the area under the jurisdiction of the Central Force, directly commanded by Rear Admiral Sanji Iwabuchi, who is also the head of the entire Manila Naval Defense Force. Expecting the main invasion of the main Allied invasion forces to come from the south of Manila and from Manila Bay, Makati was left lightly defended. 11

Santa Ana Vigilantes, 1941-1948, US Recovered PErsonnel Division, The Philippine Archives Collection, p. 17; accessed on February 6, 2024 at: http://collections.pvao.mil.ph/Collections/Guerilla/Box%20298/1430886.pdf

The Liberation Forces took advantage of this gap in the Japanese defenses to have a relatively safe crossing point between the heavily defended Fort McKinely and the dense Santa Ana suburbs.

The Aftermath

Reports say the Lissar factory was targeted and hit by artillery. 12 13 The immediate resumption of the factory’s operations shortly after the war and the structures remaining to this day suggest that the Casa Hacienda building was partially damaged but not razed.

On 9 February 1946, the manuscript on the history of Barrio Olimpia describes the first anniversary of the Liberation of the Barrio. "[It] was celebrated and the guest of honor for the occasion was Doña Trinidad L. de Roxas, wife of the then candidate for the Presidency of the Commonweaalth of the Philippines, General Manuel Roxas. Led by Mr. Pedro Calasanz, Captain Vicente Martelino and Gregorio Segovia, and several other known civic-spitited citizens, one of them Mr. Quirino Reyes, the celebration was the most colorful of its kind since the establishment of the barrio." 14
