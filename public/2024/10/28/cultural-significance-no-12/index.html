Cultural Significance No. 12

Originally published 2024-10-28

The Casa Hacienda of Makati was was part of the Sugar Empire of the Philippines.

The Casa Hacienda of Makati, formerly part of a rural estate under the ownership of the Zobel de Ayala family, evolved into a significant hub of economic activity when it became part of the operations of Victorias Milling Company (VMC). This transition marked a key moment in the history of Makati, reflecting the city's gradual transformation from agricultural land to an emerging industrial center. The presence of VMC not only connected Makati to the global sugar trade but also positioned it as a vital player in the Philippines' early industrialization.

The Sugar Industry: A Powerhouse of Philippine Growth

By the early 20th century, sugar had become one of the most important industries in the Philippines. Following World War I, the Philippine economy flourished under a model patterned after U.S. capitalism. The 1920s and 1950s were marked by rapid industrialization, with sugar playing a pivotal role in shaping the economy. In 1921, the United States facilitated the entry of Philippine sugar by waiving tariffs, leading to a boom in sugar exports. By 1934, 99% of Philippine sugar exports were sent to the U.S., compared to only 52% in 1921, making the country a global sugar powerhouse.1

The influence of the sugar industry extended far beyond economics—it brought about social, cultural, and political changes. The emergence of sugar barons, including families like the Ossorio, Elizaldes, Luzuriaga, Lopez, Ynchausti, de Leon, Gonzalez, Montillas, Aranetas, Lizareses, Cojuangco, Nepomuceno and Roxas, shaped the distribution of wealth and power in the archipelago, creating both opportunities and social divides.3

VMC's Sugar Network Expands to Makati

Established in 1919 by Miguel J. Ossorio, VMC quickly became the largest sugar producer in the Philippines, with refining facilities based in Negros Occidental. Recognizing Makati’s strategic location, VMC acquired the Casa Hacienda property sometime between the 1950s to 1980s to serve as its sugar warehouse, office, and staff housing.4

This acquisition of VMC was not Makati's first connection to the sugar industry. The Casa Hacienda property itself was once part of an agricultural estate known for sugar production during the Spanish colonial period. Under VMC, however, the estate gained new significance as a node in the company’s nationwide supply chain. Sugar harvested and refined in Negros was stored in Makati’s warehouses before being distributed to international markets.5

The Casa Hacienda played a pivotal role in VMC's operations, serving as the company's headquarters in Makati, which was starting to become the premier business address of the country at that time.

Casa Hacienda as VMC’s Hub: Work, Housing, and Community

The Casa Hacienda served not only as VMC's sales office but also provided residential accommodations for staff. In 1987, the company built a “Townhouse” complex on the property, which expanded over the years, offering housing for VMC officers and employees working in Metro Manila.6 This dual-purpose use of the estate—combining business operations with employee housing—reflected an early model of corporate urbanism that would later define Makati’s evolution into a thriving commercial hub.

The warehouses on the property formed an essential link in the sugar trade. While the estate's use as a perfume factory hinted at Makati’s nascent industrialization, its transformation under VMC marked a more deliberate shift toward heavier commercial operations.

Industrialization and Urban Growth in Makati

VMC's tenure at the Casa Hacienda reflects Makati’s transition from rural estate lands into an urban industrial center. By embedding operations like warehousing, sales, and staff housing into Makati, VMC paved the way for other corporations to establish their presence in the area. This gradual concentration of industries would ultimately transform Makati from a sleepy suburb of Manila into a premier economic hub in the decades following World War II.

The Casa Hacienda’s strategic location in Barangay Olympia played a crucial role in this shift. Situated along key roads and near waterways, the estate’s placement offered logistical advantages that attracted more businesses to the area, accelerating Makati’s industrial development.7

Bankruptcy and the End of an Era

Despite its prominence in the sugar industry, VMC faced significant financial difficulties by the 1990s. As part of the fallout from the Asian Financial Crisis, VMC declared bankruptcy in 1995, leading to a corporate restructuring that included closing several of its non-core operations, including its Manila office.8 9 In January 2008, VMC vacated the Casa Hacienda, marking the end of an era for the estate.

Following VMC’s departure, the estate faced an uncertain future. However, the City Government of Makati recognized the importance of the site, acquiring the property from the Land Bank of the Philippines in 2009. This ensured that the Casa Hacienda’s historical and cultural value would be preserved for future generations.

Why Should Makatizens Care?

The Casa Hacienda Makati stands as a testament to the city’s early industrial roots and the transformations that shaped it into the economic powerhouse it is today. While the estate may seem distant from the skyscrapers that now dominate Makati’s skyline, its story offers valuable insights into the city’s past. For Makatizens, especially those living and working in Barangay Olympia and nearby areas, understanding the role of the Casa Hacienda helps connect them to the layers of history that built their community.

Conclusion: Preserving the Casa Hacienda’s Legacy

Today, the Casa Hacienda remains a symbol of Makati’s heritage amidst rapid urbanization. Its journey from rural estate to corporate hub mirrors the broader story of the city’s transformation into a modern metropolis. As Makatizens and Filipinos continue to shape the future of Makati, the Casa Hacienda serves as a tangible reminder of the city’s industrial past and a beacon of continuity in the face of change.
